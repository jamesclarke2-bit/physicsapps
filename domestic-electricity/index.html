    The two equations

    P = I × Vpower (W) = current (A) × voltage (V)

    E = P × tenergy (J) = power (W) × time (s)

    Put the first inside the second:

    E = I × V × tenergy transferred = current × voltage × time

    Time must be in seconds. UK mains voltage is 230 V.

    Countdown

    Questions 1 to 4 are direct substitution; 5 and 6 need a rearrangement, shown as algebra in the working. "New numbers" gives a fresh set for a second class or a re-run. Question 5 is the one that matters later: current from power is the first step of choosing a fuse.

    Spec 2.6

    Two kinds of current

    A cell pushes charge one way round the circuit. The mains keeps changing its mind.

    Slowed down 100 times. Real mains completes 50 cycles every second.

    Know the difference

    • Direct current (d.c.): the current always flows in the same direction. Supplied by a cell or battery.
    • Alternating current (a.c.): the current keeps changing direction. Mains electricity is a.c.
    • UK mains: 230 V, 50 Hz. That is 50 complete cycles each second.

    a.c. or d.c.?

    Ask what they notice about the charges in the wire in a.c. mode: they never get anywhere, they shuttle to and fro. Good follow-up: "so how does the kettle still get energy?" (the heating does not depend on which way the charge moves). If you have a CRO or data-logger to hand, a cell versus a low-voltage a.c. supply makes this trace real.

    Spec 2.3

    Why a current heats a wire

    Zoom into the metal. Turn the current up and watch what the electrons do to the ions.

    2.0 A
    Wire temperature (model)20 °C
    What you would seeRoom temperature
    Demo · wire wool

    Predict: a few strands of wire wool join two crocodile clips. What will you see as the current is turned up?

    Observe: watch the colour, and watch the ammeter at the moment the strand breaks.

    Explain: use the words electrons, collide, ions and temperature.

    The explanation

    1. A current is a flow of electrons through the metal.
    2. The electrons collide with the ions of the metal lattice.
    3. Each collision transfers energy to the ions, so they vibrate more.
    4. More vibration means a higher temperature. Energy has been transferred electrically to the thermal store of the wire.

    Useful, and dangerous

    • On purpose: kettle, toaster, iron, hairdryer, electric heater, hob.
    • By accident: too much current in a cable makes it hot enough to melt its insulation and start a fire.

    So every plug carries a wire that is designed to lose this fight first.

    The temperatures are a model (heating rises with current squared), there to make the trend visible, not to be quoted. Wire wool: a few fine strands across the clips on a heatproof mat, low-voltage supply turned up from zero, eye protection, loose wool well clear. It glows, melts at its thinnest point and the ammeter drops to zero. That last observation is the bridge to the next stage: the wire wool just behaved as a fuse.

    Spec 2.2

    The fuse: a wire built to fail

    Pick a fuse, then push the current past its rating. Then try a short circuit.

    Current in the circuit2.0 A
    Rating3 A
    Fuse wirecool

    Normal current. The fuse wire stays cool.

    How a fuse protects

    1. A fuse is a thin piece of wire in the live wire.
    2. If the current rises above the fuse rating, the wire heats up and melts.
    3. The circuit is broken, so the current stops.
    4. The cable and appliance never get hot enough to start a fire.

    Circuit breakers

    A circuit breaker does the same job with an automatic switch. Compared with a fuse it:

    • cuts the current off faster
    • can be reset with a flick, so nothing needs replacing

    Ask the class

    • A 3 A fuse is carrying 2.5 A. What happens? And at 3.5 A?
    • Why not fit a 13 A fuse to everything?
    • What did the wire wool have in common with this fuse?
    The fuse wire warms as the current nears the rating and melts sooner the further you go over it: a small overload takes a second or so, a short circuit is instant. Switch to "Circuit breaker" and repeat the short circuit to show the faster trip and the reset. The "why not 13 A for everything" question sets up the quiz: a fuse far above the working current lets a damaging fault current flow without melting.

    Spec 2.4

    Pick the fuse

    Read the rating plate. Work out the current. Choose the fuse rated just above it.

    Appliance 1 of 8

    Score 0

    Streak 0

    Rating plate

    Kettle

    230 V ~ 50 Hz2500 W

    The method

    1. Write down P = I × V.
    2. Rearrange: divide both sides by V, so I = P ÷ V.
    3. Calculate the normal working current.
    4. Choose the fuse with the rating just above that current.

    Get it wrong and…

    • Rating too low: the fuse melts as soon as the appliance is switched on.
    • Rating too high: a fault current can overheat the cable and the fuse never melts.
    Eight appliances per round, drawn at random. Standard uses 3 A, 5 A and 13 A; Challenge adds 1 A and 10 A, which punishes anyone who has learned "big appliance, 13 A" without calculating. Works as whole-class voting on whiteboards (hold up 3, 5 or 13) or with students coming up to tap. "Show the current" is the scaffold and does not affect the score. The colour bands match real plug fuses: 3 A red, 5 A black, 13 A brown.

    Spec 2.2

    The earth wire: your stand-in

    Cause a fault in this metal kettle. Try it with the earth wire removed, then with it connected.

    Metal case0 V
    Current in live wire9 A
    Fuseintact

    What is happening

      The key idea

      The earth wire joins the metal case to the ground with a low-resistance path. If the case ever becomes live, a large current flows to earth and melts the fuse. The earth wire and the fuse work as a team.

      Suggested order: (1) normal working, touch the case, safe. (2) Remove the earth wire, cause a fault: nothing visible happens and the fuse is intact. Ask whether it is safe. Then touch. (3) Reset, reconnect the earth, cause the fault again and let the five steps play out. Draw out that the current through a person is far too small to melt a 13 A fuse, so a fuse alone does not protect the user. The live wire is drawn coming off the element, so the kettle also stops heating: a dead appliance can still be a live one.

      Spec 2.2

      No earth wire? Double insulation

      Some appliances have only two wires in the cable. Find out why that is still safe.

      Working normally.

      Four ways the mains is kept away from you

      FeatureWhat it isWhat it protects
      InsulationPlastic around every wire and plugThe user, from touching a live conductor
      Double insulationA plastic case as a second layerThe user: the case can never become live
      EarthingA wire from a metal case to the groundThe user, by making a fault melt the fuse
      Fuse or circuit breakerA weak link in the live wireThe cable and appliance, from overheating

      How it works

      • The wires inside are insulated: layer one.
      • The whole case is plastic, an electrical insulator: layer two.
      • Even if the live wire touches the case, the case cannot become live.
      • So no earth wire is needed. Look for the square-in-a-square symbol on the rating plate.

      Earthed or double insulated?

      Think: the French plug

      Many plugs in France have only two pins. A UK plug has three. Why is a two-pin plug not dangerous?

      1. A circuit needs only two connections: live and neutral. The third UK pin is the earth.
      2. An appliance with a two-pin plug is double insulated. Its plastic case cannot become live, so it needs no earth wire.
      3. French appliances with metal cases are still earthed. The earth pin is on the socket: it sticks out of the wall and fits into the hole in the plug.
      4. There is no fuse in a French plug. A circuit breaker in the house protects each circuit instead.
      Worth passing round a real double-insulated plug-top charger or hairdryer so they can find the symbol, and comparing its two-core flex with the three-core flex they are about to use. Common slip to listen for: "plastic stops the current" (fine) versus "plastic stops the fuse blowing" (muddled). The table is the one to copy down if you want notes from this lesson. French plug: the drawing shows the plug face, with the hole that takes the socket's earth pin. Extension for the curious: a UK plug needs its own fuse because UK ring circuits are protected at 32 A, far more than a thin flex can safely carry.

      Spec 2.2 · class practical

      Wire a plug

      Wire this one on screen first. Then do it for real.

      Pick a wire, then tap the terminal it belongs to

      You are looking into the open plug from the back. Three wires, three terminals.

      Tour of the plug

      Wire the plug correctly to unlock the tour.

      Class practical · in pairs
      1. Open the plug and loosen the cable grip.
      2. Lay the cable in place. Each wire should reach its terminal with no strain.
      3. Strip about 5 mm of insulation from each wire and twist the strands.
      4. Blue to the left, brown to the right (the fuse side), green and yellow to the top.
      5. Tighten each terminal screw. Tug each wire gently.
      6. Clamp the cable grip on the outer sheath.
      7. Check the fuse, then refit the cover.

      Swap plugs and inspect your partner's:

      A plug wired by a student must never reach a socket: use practice plugs or count them out and back in. Memory hook: bLue goes Left, bRown goes Right, and the striped one goes to the top. The cable-grip check is the one most pairs fail. The tour's earth-pin point (longer, so the earth connects first) is a nice thing to have them confirm on the real plug.

      Spec 2.2 to 2.6 · check

      Prove it

      Seven quick questions, then one longer answer.

      Exam-style question (5 marks)

      A washing machine has a metal case. The live wire works loose and touches the case.

      Explain how the earth wire and the fuse protect the user.

      1. The metal case becomes live.
      2. The earth wire gives a low-resistance path from the case to earth.
      3. A large current flows through the live wire, the case and the earth wire.
      4. This current is greater than the fuse rating, so the fuse heats up and melts.
      5. The circuit is broken, so the case is no longer live and nobody touching it gets a shock.

      Countdown

      The seven questions map to the spec points: 1 and 7 to 2.2, 2 to 2.4, 3 to 2.2, 4 to 2.6, 5 to 2.5, 6 to 2.3. The longer question is written for this lesson and is not from a past paper; the five mark points are the same chain the earth-wire simulation steps through. A good homework is to give them the same question for a hairdryer with a plastic case.
